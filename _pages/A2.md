## 1. Background of the Text

*1 the Road* is a 2018 experimental novel created by an artificial neural network, and conceptualized and executed by Ross Goodwin, a creative technologist and MIT graduate. In March 2017, Goodwin traveled from New York to New Orleans in a Cadillac, in order to deliberately trace the iconic mid-century American road trip immortalized by Jack Kerouac’s *On the Road*. Yet, the "author" of this modern journey was a laptop running a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network, which was fed a continuous stream of sensory input from a surveillance camera mounted on the trunk, a GPS unit, a microphone inside the cabin, and a computer clock. And, as the vehicle moved, the AI processed this data and generated a real-time narrative printed directly onto continuous rolls of receipt paper.

The text that comes about from this process is a disjointed, highly fragmented patchwork of raw and more observational prose with precise geographic coordinates, time stamps, and place names. In addition, the source of this AI's spatial awareness and vocabulary were heavily influenced by the Foursquare API. Since Foursquare is a commercial platform driven by user check-ins, the AI's perception of the American landscape was consequently aggressively skewed toward consumer waypoints. This means the novel strips away human reflection and showcases a landscape focused on gas stations, restaurants, and other consumption oriented places. This was intentional: in replacing human romanticism with algorithmic pattern recognition, Goodwin wanted to create a text that forces readers to think deeper on how ML models perceive, document, and finally construct human spaces.

## 2. Downloading and Cleaning

We downloaded the Recogito export of `1TheRoad_sectioned.txt` as a CSV on October 31. The cleaned version is in the [assets/A2 folder](https://github.com/I-brohim/dhs/tree/master/assets/A2).

When I first loaded the raw export, only 81 out of 246 rows could be mapped. The coordinates were mostly there, just in the comments column. So the main cleaning was moving 159 coordinates from comments and 4 from tags into LAT/LNG. We also found a mistake of our own: for Patapsco State Park we typed 76 instead of -76, and the point ended up in Kazakhstan.

![number_of_mappable_coords]({{ '/assets/A2/number_of_mappable_coords.png' | relative_url }})
*Notebook output before cleaning: only 81 of the 246 manual rows had usable coordinates.*

Then I looked at the points far from the route. At first I thought GeoNames made them up, but the text does mention these names. GeoNames just picks the most prominent place with that name, so I used the neighbouring places in the text instead:

- Arizona → Arizona Avenue in Baltimore, since the points around it are in Baltimore.
- San Jose → a San Jose Mexican Restaurant near Raleigh, not the city in California.
- Ashwood and 5 of the 10 Columbias → South Carolina, not Tennessee, because Manville, SC is right next to them in the text.
- Winchester → I-285 in Atlanta, not the UK. Everything around it is in Atlanta.

![us_manual_uncleaned_with_east_cost_and_arizona_and_tn]({{ '/assets/A2/us_manual_uncleaned_with_east_cost_and_arizona_and_tn.png' | relative_url }})
*Manual layer before cleaning: the route along the East Coast, with stray points in California (San Jose), Arizona and Tennessee.*

Belgium was the one I could not solve, so we cleared its coordinates instead of guessing. Together with "Biloxiana Parker", which looks like noise from the text generation, that leaves 244 out of 246 rows mappable, with no rows deleted.

What I find interesting is that I fixed these points the same way the automated tools got them wrong. They chose the biggest Columbia, I chose the one that made sense for the road trip. I think I am more aware of context, more aware than machines. So the "cleaned" manual layer is not the truth either, it is our best interpretation of it.

## 4. Analysis

### Interpretation vs Automation

When we map the geographic entities extracted from the text, a clear contrast emerges between human and computer. As we can see in the map, human annotation creates a logical and dense path along the United States East Coast and down into the South, which accurately reflects the physical journey from New York to New Orleans. In contrast, the automated methods (spaCy/geopy and the LLM) scatter points globally, identifying locations in Europe, South America, and Australia.

![world]({{ '/assets/A2/world.png' | relative_url }})
*All three layers zoomed out: the automated points (orange and green) spread across Europe, South America, Africa, Asia and Australia, while the manual layer stays in the US.*

This discrepancy shows the decisions human annotators make that automated systems cannot do. For example, I rely on narrative and spatial context to deduce that places like “Winchester” or “Belgium” mentioned during a drive through the American South are likely local street names, commercial signs, or probably just camera attached to the car seeing these texts in posters, signs and putting them in, misinterpreting them as geographic entities. Automated systems, since they’re relying strictly on named entity recognition and gazetteers like GeoNames, lack this contextual awareness. These sorts of scripts assume that if a string of text matches a place name in a database, it should be mapped to its most prominent global coordinates.

The extracted row counts further reveal the aggressive over-reach of automation: namely, whereas the human annotator mapped 245 verifiable locations with only 1 unmappable error, spaCy extracted 341 points and the LLM extracted 374\. Both machine models failed entirely on 93 of their extracted rows. The multiple levels of automation, spanning from the AI generating the text, to the OCR software introducing textual noise, to the LLM's uncritical NER extraction, compound together to create a version of space that is heavily abstracted. As a result, visual mapping adjustments, such as adding jitter to prevent points from occulting each other, become very necessary to help decipher this automated data and reveal the magnitude of the machine's deviation from reality.

### Critical Questions

The process of mapping *1 the Road* showcases the inherent limits of computational approaches to spatial data. Specifically, automated geocoding systems always carry the risk of systematically misrepresenting locations due to defaulting to standardized datasets, thereby stripping away localized nuance and human context. This dynamic also perfectly illustrates the concerns raised by digital humanities scholar Johanna Drucker regarding the nature of information and visualization. Drucker argues that humanists must reject the concept of “data” (which implies information is a natural, pre-existing given) and instead recognize information as “capta” (something which is actively taken and constructed through interpretation).

In our case, when Goodwin's Wordcar pulled spatial data from the Foursquare API, it did not passively record an objective and observer-independent landscape. Instead, it constructed this very specific version of the world that prioritized commercial businesses and consumer waypoints. As mentioned before, the spatial information generated by the AI was heavily biased by the corporate APIs it relied upon, which just perfectly demonstrates Drucker's point that no data point exists in a vacuum and what gets measured is determined by the biases and rules of the system recording it.

Moreover, Drucker points out that maps create a false illusion of total accuracy and mask how subjective and ambiguous human space actually is. So, certainly the automated mapping of Goodwin’s text successfully plots exact latitudes and longitudes, but at the same time it entirely fails to capture the narrative logic of the physical journey. Because the LLM and spaCy lack contextual awareness, they treat OCR errors and ambiguous words as literal geographic coordinates, and in doing so project a false global scale onto a regional American road trip. The comparison between the human and machine maps proves that without humanistic interpretation, relying purely on automated extraction yields a dataset that may look more expansive but is actually quite hollow.
